The internet wasn't always like this.

We weren't sorted into bottomless content pits based on our attentive properties. It didn't haunt our pockets, it was a physical place we went to, at a desk in the corner of our rooms. We used the internet to make connections, not reinforce divisions. Before the decline into monopolization by the technocrats, people were excited to be riding the information superhighway.

Even the term “information superhighway” comes off naive and utopian today. Doomscroll, rage bait, brain rot, engagement farm, terminally online, slop, outrage economy: this is the lens through which we now view the internet. Nobody's talking about hypertext, surfing the web, netizens, the electronic frontier... Why? What's changed?

<hr/>

dunedinmusic.com came into my life because people kept confusing it with my site. They still do, but the similarity is entirely coincidental, I didn't know of it before I registered my domain. I am of the age where I saw the rise of forums during my early online years, then saw them steadily replaced by modern corporate social media. So by the time I became aware of dunedinmusic.com, the forum era had passed and it was a shell of what it once was.

But I find it fascinating that there was this little mostly forgotten hyper-local community which rose and fell just before I moved here.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['forum-screenshot-2']} caption="dunedinmusic.com forum in 2007" objectFit="contain"/>

The dunedinmusic.com domain was registered on the 13th of March 2003. It was the project of Evan Sunley and Scott Muir.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['r1-dmc-2007-quiznight-evan-and-scott']} caption="Left to right: Evan Sunley, Robert Milne, idk, and Scott Muir (2007)" maxWidth="400px"/>

Its stated purpose was to be a home for the music community in Dunedin. It hosted a gig guide, artist profiles, venue profiles, gig reviews, news, even a store for buying local music.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['about']} alt="dunedinmusic.com about page" maxWidth="600px"/>

And for a long time it fulfilled this purpose. DMC (as it became known) was firmly embedded in the local music community, becoming a hub for discussion, promotion, networking. It also held some legendary birthday gigs.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['2006-martin-philips-dm-first-birthday']} caption="Martin Phillips plays DMC's first birthday (2006)"/>

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['dm-second-birthday-all-ages-2']} caption="DMC's second birthday all-ages gig (2007)"/>

It later became a label of sorts, helping to print and distribute music for local bands. For this article though I'm focusing on the forum.

In 2005 the forum had 366 registered users and 7,618 posts. In just a few years this rocketed to 1,065 users and 23,946 posts, and by its peak it had 2,514 members and 37,636 posts. And unlike today, these users weren't simply whipping out their phones to tap out a message, the netizens of Dunedin were firing up their 56k modems and logging into cyberspace.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['user-count-graph']} alt="Graph of user count over time" maxWidth="600px"/>

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['post-count-graph']} caption="The site was relaunched in 2014 and old posts purged, hence the drop" maxWidth="600px"/>

It's hard to fathom just how different the experience of “going online” was back in 2006. It wasn't until 2007 that [broadband overtook dial-up use in New Zealand](https://www3.stats.govt.nz/New_Zealand_Official_Yearbooks/2008/NZOYB_2008.html#idsect1_1_64700), and mobile internet was slow and expensive, mostly limited to downloading polyphonic ringtones on WAP for a couple of bucks a pop.

So for the vast majority of people, using the internet meant sitting at a computer, unleashing a scree of beeps and boops while it dials up, and tying up the phoneline. Compared to the 300mbps+ download speed most people get on fibre today, dial-up was only 56kbps (or 0.056mbps). 

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['upload-issues']} caption="People on Windows XP with dial up were trying to upload MP3s, unsuccessfully" objectFit="contain"/>

These technological limitations informed the design. There were fewer images because images were expensive to download, and more room for text because that's the primary reason why people were there. Screens were lower resolution, so interfaces were busier because there were less pixels to work with. And because designers only had to consider mouse cursors, not fingers, click targets didn't need to be as big.

Despite this though it feels roomier to me. The overuse of padding and whitespace in modern web design leads to a viewport which is mostly full of nothing, which in turn leads to the sense that I'm looking at everything through a pair of ski goggles. Minimalism doesn't equal simplicity, even if it might seem that way at first.

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['off-topic-forum']} alt="Forum screenshot" objectFit="contain"/>

This sort of density, in my opinion, is a better user experience (at least on desktop), although I know not everyone will agree.

What I want to know is whether my fondness towards this era is just rose-tinted nostalgia, or if we have actually lost something. What can we learn from revisiting conversations from the webforum era? To try and answer this I scraped thousands of forum pages from the archive.org snapshots, and sifted through them for clues.

<h2>Ethics</h2>

While these are all public posts, and they're from decades ago, the people who wrote them did so within a certain context and didn't necessarily expect their posts to be published beyond the bounds of the forum. In consideration of this I've generated random pseudonyms for usernames (with a few exceptions for public figures) and only resurfaced posts for reasons of legitimate interest. I'm not here to gotcha anyone. 

These are recreations of the original posts, and some have been edited for brevity. I've also included relevant images harvested from the galleries section of the website.

## 2007: The music industry has always sucked

<Image size="blog" responsiveImage={frontmatter.responsiveImages['2007-usercount']} alt="Graph of forum user count" maxWidth="600px"/>

The early days of the forum preceded the rise of streaming services. It existed in an awkward transition phase, where CD sales still dominated but people were increasingly listening to music on iPods and other MP3 players. Online music marketplaces existed, but the value proposition didn't seem to appeal. People would reluctantly fork over $30 for a CD, but $30 for some files on a computer didn't feel right.

As you'd expect, the industry was reluctant to release its chokehold on music sales, and there was much contempt from musicians for this behemoth of an industry which transparently cared about musicians far less than the products they create. Which prompted a forum user to make this thread.

This is largely what ended up happening. More people can record and release music than ever before, and there are fewer barriers between the musicians and their audience. Record stores did stick around though, albeit as boutique specialist shops rather than something you find in every mall.

But musicians still aren't really getting paid very well. Consumers aren't spending $30 on a CD, of which the artist might see a few dollars. Instead they're spending $20 a month on a subscription to all music ever released, of which artists are seeing fractions of a cent per play.

The power has shifted slightly, but those at the top have made sure they still hold the cards.

And of course, at this point nobody could predict the resurgence of vinyl as the leading physical format, which in 2007 had been steadily declining for decades. It is important to note though that even with vinyl's surprise comeback, physical media sales today are still a fraction of what they were. 

In 2007, people who wanted to listen to music bought a CD. Today, people who want to listen to music stream it. Some buy vinyl, but vinyl purchases are a footnote in terms of music sales, largely driven by collectors and fanatics, [half of who don't own turntables](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/50-of-vinyl-buyers-dont-own-a-record-player-data-shows/).

I also wonder what impact the pseudonymous nature of the early internet has on these sorts of interactions? Before platforms like Facebook it was generally advised NOT to use your real name online, but at some point there was an aggressive push towards associating every online user with their real life identity.

The idea was real names create accountability, people will be more likely to behave if their actions are associated with their real life identity. Behind the scenes it was probably also to encourage people to reveal more of their “real” selves online so their data could be sold to advertisers.

But I think the opposite happened, it killed authentic interactions. People are able to act more authentically in online spaces when they have full autonomy over how they’re perceived. And a quick scan through any Facebook comments section reveals that people are more than willing to behave badly under their real names.

## Conclusions

Going through these archives has been fascinating for me, all the little stories which are perfectly preserved thanks to the tireless work of [the internet archive](https://archive.org). Everything that's changed, and everything that hasn't. 

It's a window into a very different world. Despite not being that long ago, the technological and economic landscape of the internet has changed significantly with its explosive growth. We were once in control of how and when the online world moved forward, if we didn't refresh the page it would stay still. Today the online world moves us.

Users wanted a place to interact with their friends and migrated to social media platforms, but were quickly sorted into silos and drowned in slop. By then it was too late, dead forums were abandoned by their owners and there was nowhere to go back to.

In 2004, Mckenzie Wark published “A Hacker Manifesto” in which she defined the two opposing forces of information in the internet era: vectoralists and hackers. Vectoralists seek to control and commodify information, while the hackers are the creators of novelty.

This has largely come to pass. The vectors through which information is shared have been capitalized by the factory owners of the 21st century. Now the hackers have little choice but to put themselves up for exploitation. It's the difference between original content posted to a forum owned and controlled by a guy up the road, and original content posted on Reddit, then passed to another party to train a large language model for a billion dollar company.

Users posting on a platform where the owners are available, active, and clearly members of the community incentivizes positive interactions more than being in a giant pool of strangers on a platform where you are a point of data, and everything you do feeds into some giant faceless machine. Not that dunedinmusic.com wasn't monetising its users, but running ads for local businesses is a far cry from what companies like Meta do.

Whether consciously or subconsciously, people weren't trying to optimize their sentences for maximum impact to compete for attention in a small space, they were just saying stuff. Sometimes lots of stuff. And other people were reading it all, and taking the time to respond with their own stuff. And they were all guaranteed to be humans poking at buttons with their fingers rather than machines predicting tokens.

If your interface is geared around what people are saying, then they'll say more. If your interface is geared around reactions, then you'll get shallow takes.

If the platform opaquely controls the visibility of your interactions, which communities see them, and why they see them, then it's no wonder people don't feel social on social media, despite sharing a platform with literally billions of others.

### What's next?

It’s important to acknowledge what hasn’t changed. We still have a local music community. We are all still people. People still like to congregate and connect with each other.

What has changed is how we do this. In the early days, the internet was a wild new frontier for people to set up their own little villages. Today it's mostly been colonized by corporates, and we're living in corporate towns.

The vectoralists don't want you to know this but there are people trying to fix this problem. Many still believe in the dream of the internet, and reject the rotten new reality corrupted by capital.

Groups like [IndieWeb](https://indieweb.org/) champion open protocols like ActivityPub, which have been created to undo this mess and foster “a people-focused alternative to the corporate web”. You can help take back the web by supporting independent platforms, or making your own wee corner of the web like this one. It's hard to break these chains because of the [network effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_effect), but we should at least *try* to escape the major platforms.

All of this was only possible due to the incredible work done by [the internet archive](https://archive.org) to preserve internet history. You should [donate to the Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://www.eff.org/) if you can.
